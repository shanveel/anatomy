A system built for one meeting

The female reproductive system is a set of internal organs, from the ovaries to the vagina, plus the external structures of the vulva. Together they do five jobs. Tap an organ to find it on the diagram.

  • Makes eggsThe ovaries mature and release one egg each cycle.
  • Makes hormonesEstrogen and progesterone run the monthly cycle.
  • Hosts fertilizationSperm and egg meet in the fallopian tube.
  • Supports pregnancyThe uterus shelters and nourishes the embryo.
  • Gives birthThe uterus contracts; the vagina becomes the birth canal.

  • Two almond-sized glands, one on each side of the uterus. Each egg waits inside a follicle, set in supporting tissue called the stroma. The ovaries release one egg each cycle and make estrogen and progesterone.

  • Each tube has four parts. The fimbriae and funnel-shaped infundibulum catch the released egg. The wide ampulla is where fertilization usually happens, and the narrow isthmus joins the uterus. Tiny hair-like cilia and gentle muscle contractions move the egg along.

  • A hollow, muscular organ about the size of a pear. Its domed top is the fundus and its main part is the body. It has three layers: the endometrium lining, the thick myometrium muscle, and a thin outer perimetrium. The muscle stretches during pregnancy and contracts during birth.

  • The lining of the uterus. It thickens each cycle to receive an embryo. If no embryo implants, its upper layer is shed as the menstrual period.

  • The narrow lower end of the uterus. Its cervical canal opens into the vagina. Its mucus turns thin and slippery around ovulation, which helps sperm pass through.

  • An elastic muscular canal from the cervix to the outside of the body. It receives sperm during intercourse, carries menstrual flow out of the body, and stretches to become the birth canal.

  • The external genital structures: the labia majora and labia minora, the clitoris, and the vestibule around the vaginal and urethral openings. They protect the internal organs.

Inside the ovary: making an egg

Making eggs is called oogenesis. It starts before birth, then pauses until puberty. From then on, a few follicles start to grow each cycle, and usually just one finishes.

  1. Primordial follicle

    An immature egg wrapped in a single layer of flat cells. Almost all of the 1–2 million at birth stay at this stage.

  2. Growing follicle

    Each cycle FSH wakes a few follicles. Layers of cells multiply around the egg, and the zona pellucida forms.

  3. Mature (Graafian) follicle

    A fluid-filled space, the antrum, swells the follicle to about 2 cm. It makes most of the cycle’s estrogen.

  4. Ovulation

    The follicle bursts at the ovary’s surface and releases the egg, still wrapped in its corona radiata.

  5. Corpus luteum

    The empty follicle turns into a yellow gland that makes progesterone. Without a pregnancy it fades after about 14 days.

Hormones

The four hormones

  • FSH follicle-stimulating hormone

    From the pituitary gland, at the base of the brain

    Makes follicles in the ovary grow, so they start producing estrogen.

  • LH luteinizing hormone

    From the pituitary gland

    Its surge triggers ovulation. Afterwards it supports the corpus luteum as it makes progesterone.

  • Estrogen also spelled oestrogen

    From the growing follicles

    Rebuilds the uterine lining and thins cervical mucus. It also drives breast development at puberty and supports bone and heart health.

  • Progesterone

    From the corpus luteum, later the placenta

    Prepares and maintains the lining for pregnancy. When it falls, the lining is shed as a period.

Two cells become one

Of the millions of sperm released, a few hundred reach the egg in the ampulla of the fallopian tube. Only one gets in.

The sperm’s journey

  1. VaginaMillions of sperm arrive. Its acidic environment kills many of them.
  2. CervixAround ovulation its mucus turns thin and stretchy, and lets thousands of the strongest swimmers through.
  3. UterusSperm swim up through the cavity, helped along by its muscle contractions.
  4. Fallopian tubeThey enter through the narrow isthmus. Chemical signals from the egg (chemotaxis) guide them on.
  5. AmpullaA few hundred reach the egg, and fertilization happens here.

Fertilization, step by step

  1. Sperm reach the egg

    Sperm swim through the cervix and uterus into the tube, and push through the corona radiata, the layer of follicle cells around the egg.

  2. The acrosome reaction

    Enzymes released from the acrosome, a cap on the sperm head, digest a path through the zona pellucida, the egg's glassy coat.

  3. One sperm fuses

    The first sperm to fuse with the egg membrane triggers the cortical reaction. The zona hardens into a fertilization membrane that blocks polyspermy: no other sperm can get in.

  4. A zygote forms

    The egg finishes its second meiotic division. The male and female pronuclei, each with 23 chromosomes, combine. The zygote has 46, restoring the diploid (2n) number.

Sperm cell

  • Head: holds the acrosome and the nucleus
  • Acrosome: enzyme-filled cap on the head
  • Nucleus: 23 chromosomes from the father
  • Middle piece (midpiece): packed with mitochondria that power swimming
  • Tail (flagellum): whips to drive the cell forward

Egg cell (ovum)

  • Corona radiata: outer layer of follicle cells
  • Zona pellucida: protective glycoprotein coat
  • Cell membrane: where the sperm fuses
  • Cytoplasm: nutrients for the first cell divisions
  • Nucleus: 23 chromosomes from the mother

The first week

While it travels down the tube, the zygote divides again and again without growing. Day 0 is the day of fertilization.

  1. Day 0

    Zygote Ampulla

    A single cell with a full set of 46 chromosomes.

  2. Day 1–2

    Cleavage Fallopian tube

    The first division happens about 30 hours after fertilization. The cells, called blastomeres, get smaller with each split.

  3. Day 3–4

    Morula Entering uterus

    A solid ball of 16 or more cells that looks like a mulberry. It reaches the uterus around day 4.

  4. Day 5

    Blastocyst Uterus

    A fluid-filled ball. Its inner cell mass becomes the embryo; the outer trophoblast will help form the placenta.

  5. Day 6–10

    Implantation Endometrium

    The blastocyst burrows into the endometrium and starts making hCG. This hormone keeps the corpus luteum producing progesterone, so the lining is not shed. Pregnancy tests detect hCG.

Caring for the system

Knowing how the system works makes it easier to look after, and to notice when something is off.

Common conditions

PCOS (polycystic ovary syndrome)
A hormone imbalance that can cause irregular periods, acne, extra hair growth and weight gain, and can make it harder to get pregnant.
Endometriosis
Tissue similar to the uterine lining grows outside the uterus, causing painful periods and pelvic pain.
Fibroids
Non-cancerous growths in the muscle of the uterus that can cause heavy or painful periods.

Good to know

  • Some period pain is common. Pain that stops you from going about your day is not normal, and is worth seeing a doctor about.
  • The HPV vaccine protects against the virus behind most cervical cancers.
  • Regular cervical screening (the Pap smear) finds early cell changes in the cervix, long before they cause harm.
  • A cycle that suddenly changes or stops is worth getting checked.

Everyday self-care

  • Drink enough water
  • Eat a balanced diet with iron-rich foods
  • Move regularly
  • Sleep well and manage stress
  • Track your cycle to learn your pattern
  • Go for routine check-ups

Your body is not a problem to be fixed. It’s a system to be understood and cared for.
